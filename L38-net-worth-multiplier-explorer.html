Net worth as a multiplier

A euro of balance sheet moves more than a euro of investment — and then it moves the economy.

The firm funds investment from its own net worth plus borrowing, I = w + b, and a lender will advance at most b ≤ λw. So I = min{ I*, (1+λ)w }, with the constraint binding below w* = I*/(1+λ). The frictionless benchmark says ∂I/∂w = 0 — exactly zero, the balance sheet is irrelevant. Move the sliders and watch what replaces it.

Try to answer

  1. Nudge w just below w*. Why does ∂I/∂w jump from 0 to 1+λ rather than rising smoothly?
  2. That jump is a famous objection to a whole literature. Whose, and what does it say about sorting firms into "constrained" and "unconstrained"?
  3. Raise λ from the lecture case. Investment rises — but so does the amplification. Is better collateral good news?
  4. Raise φ until the loop stops converging. What is the economy doing at that point, and what would stop it?

Two movements carry this lecture

Nudging a parameter and reading off which way investment moves is comparative statics — and it is what an empirical paper's headline coefficient estimates. Most of what these sliders do is arithmetic. These two are not.

1 · A euro of net worth moves more than a euro — or nothing at all
—
Not a slope but a step: drag w across w* and it switches. Chaney–Sraer–Thesmar (2012) estimate it off real-estate shocks — about six cents per euro of collateral — and Almeida–Campello (2007) show the sensitivity itself rises with tangibility.
2 · Aggregated, it can run away
—
One firm's constraint is a number; every firm's constraint is a loop, because the collateral is a traded asset. Kiyotaki–Moore (1997) put it in general equilibrium.

One thing to carry away

You cannot see w* in data, so you cannot tell which side of it a firm sits on — which is exactly Kaplan–Zingales's (1997) objection: sort firms into "constrained" and "unconstrained" badly enough and the step above turns into noise. That is why the credible designs reach for something that moves the constraint from outside the firm — house prices, an accounting rule.
