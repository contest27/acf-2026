The put the taxpayer wrote

Why an insured bank wants more risk, and what capital does about it.

A bank holds assets worth 100 on average, funded with capital w and insured deposits D = 100 − w. Assets pay 100 − s or 100 + s, equally likely — so sliding s changes the risk and nothing else; expected assets stay at 100 throughout. Depositors are made whole whatever happens, which means somebody else pays max(D − A, 0): a put nobody sold and, for sixty years, nobody priced. Its value is S.

Two movements carry this lecture

Move one slider and watch a number move: that is comparative statics, and it is the whole method. These two are the ones worth remembering.

1 · Capital only bites once it survives the bad state
Silicon Valley Bank held 94% of its deposits above the insured limit, against 41% at comparable banks — and it was the uninsured depositors who ran.
2 · A flat premium taxes the prudent bank
US deposit insurance was flat-rate from 1933 until 1 January 1993, when it moved to nine risk classes priced on capital and supervisory rating.
