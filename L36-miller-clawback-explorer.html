Miller's clawback

The friction you were surest about — and how it changes sign.

Route one euro of income to investors. As debt they keep (1−τd). As equity they keep (1−τc)(1−τe) — after all three taxes. With permanent debt the gain from leverage is the ratio of the two survival rates: GL/D = 1 − (1−τc)(1−τe)/(1−τd). Your bachelor course counted only the corporate layer — a flat τc, independent of τd. Move the sliders and watch where that goes wrong.

Try to answer

  1. Set τd = τe. Why does the gain collapse to exactly τc — the bachelor's answer?
  2. Set τe = 0 (all equity return as untaxed deferred gains). What does τd* become, and what is Miller's point?
  3. Raise τc. The gain rises — but so does τd*. Which effect does a government control?

Two movements carry this lecture

Nudging a tax rate and reading off which way the gain moves is comparative statics — and it is what an exam question asks for. Most of what these sliders do is arithmetic. These two are not.

1 · Taxing interest destroys the shield
—
The movement the bachelor's τcD says does not exist — it makes the gain flat in τd. Getting this one wrong is what makes the friction look safe.
2 · The flip point moves with the corporate rate
—
Raise τc and the gain rises — but the rate at which it vanishes rises too. With τe = 0 this is exactly Miller: τd* = τc, and leverage is a wash in aggregate.

One thing to carry away

Almost nothing in this formula is observable. τe is an effective rate blending dividends and deferred gains; τd is set in equilibrium. So the literature estimates the whole object instead — Korteweg (2010) recovers the net benefit directly and finds it hump-shaped, with the median firm below the optimum. That is a weaker claim than testing any one of these movements, and the difference is worth noticing.
