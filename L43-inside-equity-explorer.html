The manager's stake

Why a dividend waits — and why some firms never get to pay one.

The manager's stake W is the only thing standing between him and liquidation, and it moves with the cash flow because it has to. Hit the floor and the firm is wound up. Hit the ceiling and cash is paid out. The boundary is normalised to 1, so W₀ below is read as a fraction of it — a young firm starts low, a mature one starts high. Everything you see is the same contract at different points.

Two readings

Move one slider, watch one number move, and say which way and by how much. These two are the ones worth carrying out of the room.

1. How long a dividend takes
Payout is not a policy the firm follows — it is what happens when the stake reaches the ceiling. So “when does this firm start paying dividends?” is a question about accumulation, and the answer is a number of years rather than a payout ratio.
2. How many never get there
This is the cross-section DeAngelo, DeAngelo and Stulz measured, generated one path at a time. Firms whose equity is mostly earned pay; firms whose equity is mostly contributed mostly do not — and some of them are simply not around to.
Model. W ← W + γW·dt + λσ√dt·ε, absorbed at 0, reflected at 1; 400 seeded paths, monthly steps, 30-year horizon. The ceiling is taken as given, not solved — solving for it is the HJB problem of DeMarzo–Sannikov and is well beyond this course. What the full model says about where it sits: a longer distance to the ceiling for riskier cash flows, a shorter one when the firm is worth more dead (DS06, Table I). The effect of λ on that distance is, in their word, complex — so this Explorer lets you move λ and the ceiling independently rather than pretending to a sign the paper does not claim.
