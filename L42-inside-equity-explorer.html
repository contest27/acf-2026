Where the dividend starts

The payout boundary is not a policy. It is the solution to a problem — so move the problem and watch it move.

The manager’s stake W is the only thing standing between him and liquidation, and it moves with the cash flow because it has to. Hit the floor and the firm is wound up; hit the ceiling and cash is paid out. This page does not put that ceiling anywhere — it solves for it, along with the point the contract starts him at. Everything is measured per euro of capital, so w̄ = 0.6 means he may build a claim worth sixty cents per euro of capital before a dividend is paid.

Three readings

Move one slider at a time. These are the three sentences worth carrying out of the room — each of them is a number on this page that you can make move.

1. The ceiling is chosen
Nobody wrote a payout rule. The boundary is where a euro kept inside the firm stops being worth more than a euro paid out — and that point depends on the friction, so payout policy is a consequence of the agency problem, not a decision taken beside it.
2. Two measures of slack, moving apart
λ and σ enter the problem only as the product λσ, so they move the manager’s buffer identically. But the thing you can read off a balance sheet is the credit line, w̄/λ — and it moves the other way. Two firms with the same buffer can look completely different to an analyst.
3. What average q gets wrong
The gap is w(1+p′): zero at the floor, zero at the ceiling where p′ = −1, and widest in between. So the measurement error in a q-regression is not a constant to be absorbed by a fixed effect — it is a function of the state variable the regression left out.

The same solution, read as investment

The value function already contains the investment policy: the first-order condition says i = (qm − 1)/θ with marginal q equal to p − w p′. So no second model is needed — this is the deck’s second figure, derived rather than drawn.

And what it does to a cohort

A thousand firms, all written on this contract: each starts at the solved w0* and runs until its stake reaches one of the two boundaries. Nothing here is a free choice — the starting point and the ceiling both came from the value function above.

Model. DeMarzo, Fishman, He and Wang (2012), scaled by capital, with DeMarzo–Sannikov (2006) as the no-investment case. The investors’ value p(w) solves r p = μ − g(i) + (i−δ)[p − w p′] + γ w p′ + ½(λσ)² p″ with g(i) = i + θi²/2, subject to p(0) = ℓ, p′(w̄) = −1 and p″(w̄) = 0. The third condition is super-contact: it is what makes the boundary optimal rather than imposed, and it is why this page can claim to solve for w̄ rather than assume it. Solved by backward RK4 from the boundary with bisection on w̄. Technology, held fixed: r = 5%, δ = 10%, θ = 6, μ = 0.215, giving a first-best q = 1.72 and 2% first-best growth. Only the four frictions move. Cohort: dw = w(γ − i(w) + δ)dt + λσ dZ, absorbed at 0; at the ceiling a dividend is paid and the stake steps back to 0.9 w̄. 1,000 seeded paths, monthly steps, 30-year horizon. Every displayed share is a first-hit statistic, so none of them depends on the size of that step-down. Ungraded self-practice.
