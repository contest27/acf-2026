The manager wedge

Why more equity can make a manager more cautious — and what convex pay does about it.

Shareholders are diversified: they want any project with G ≥ 0, at every stake. The manager is not. On a contract w = α + βy he weighs his share of the value against the risk he is made to carry, and — if he also holds options — against the variance those options are paid for: CE(β) = βG − kβ² + ν·Δσ², with k = (r/2)·Δσ². He takes the project iff CE ≥ 0. The gap between his rule and theirs is the wedge.

Try to answer

  1. From the lecture case, drag β right. His appetite falls. What is the board buying with that extra equity, and who pays for it?
  2. With ν = 0, raise Δσ² — he gets worse off. Now set ν = 0.030 and raise it again. What changed, and why is that the point of paying in options?
  3. Set G negative and raise ν until he accepts. Who pays for that project?

Two movements carry this lecture

Nudging a parameter and reading off which way the answer moves is comparative statics — and it is what an exam question asks for, and what an empirical paper's headline coefficient estimates. Most of the movements here are arithmetic. These two are not.

1 · More equity makes him more cautious

The result you should not have expected, and the reason β = 1 is a corner to avoid rather than an ideal to fall short of.
2 · Options flip his attitude to risk
—
This is vega. Coles–Daniel–Naveen (2006) measure it; Bakke et al. (2016) identify it causally off the FAS 123R shock — take the convexity away and hedging jumps.
